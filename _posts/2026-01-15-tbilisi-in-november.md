The flight from Vienna lands in darkness. Tbilisi's airport sits east of the city in a flat bowl of land, and the taxi ride in takes you past Soviet apartment blocks whose windows glow amber against the black sky. By the time you reach the old town — Abanotubani, the sulfur-bath district — it is past midnight and the streets smell of stone and warm mineral water.

## Day One: The Baths and the Narikala

The bathhouses open early. I arrived at Chreli-Abano just after seven, paid twelve lari for a private room, and spent an hour in water hot enough to make your thoughts go soft. The walls are tiled in blue and white. A tap drips. Outside, the city slowly wakes.

After the baths I climbed to Narikala. The fortress is a ruin — earthquake, neglect, time — but the walls still stand well enough to frame the view. The Mtkvari river below looks grey-green in November light. On the far bank the new city stretches away in glass and concrete; on this bank, Abanotubani's domed bathhouses cluster like mushrooms between the alleys.

A man was selling churchkhela from a wooden cart near the upper gate. I bought two — walnut, grape-juice-dipped — and ate them on the wall looking south.

## Day Two: Rue Leselidze and a Wine Bar

The main artery of the old town changes names several times. I walked its full length — Leselidze, then Kote Afkhazi — past the Orthodox cathedral, past a Soviet-era bookshop still selling Russian paperbacks, past a new third-wave coffee place that could have been in Berlin. Tbilisi absorbs contradiction without apparent strain.

Georgian wine is older than most European wine traditions by several thousand years. The qvevri — large clay vessels buried in the earth — are still used by small producers in Kakheti. Amber wine, made from white grapes with extended skin contact, is the result: tannin-forward, oxidative, strange to palates trained on French whites.

I drank a glass of Rkatsiteli at a wine bar on Aghmashenebeli. The owner, a woman in her sixties, explained in careful English that her family had kept vines in Kakheti for five generations. The Soviet period interrupted the tradition. She resumed it in 1994.

## Day Three: The Dry Bridge Market and Departure

Sunday mornings at the Dry Bridge bring out the city's attic. Vendors spread Soviet medals, painted icons, battered samovars, mismatched cutlery, and hand-knitted socks across folding tables and blankets. I spent two hours walking the rows.

![Vendors at the Dry Bridge flea market](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5d/Dry_Bridge_market_Tbilisi.jpg/1280px-Dry_Bridge_market_Tbilisi.jpg){: .float-left}

*Sunday morning at the Dry Bridge. Everything the city no longer needs.*{: .caption-left}

I bought a small oil painting — a mountain scene, unframed, unsigned — for twenty lari. The vendor wrapped it in newspaper without comment. A few stalls down, an elderly man was selling pre-war German postcards. He spoke no English; I spoke no Georgian. We looked at them together anyway.

The flight home left at four. By five I was above the clouds, the painting in the overhead bin, the smell of sulfur water already fading from memory.

> Tbilisi does not try to charm you. It simply continues to exist, in its own way, on its own terms. That, in the end, is charm enough.
